## 🎬 Lesson-by-Lesson Teaching Notes

### **Lesson 1: Vital Signs in Crisis — What the Numbers Mean**
**Duration:** 12 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Up

**Learning Goal:** Students recognize and name vital sign deterioration.

**Key Phrases to Emphasize:**
- SpO2 <90% = **critical hypoxia** (requires immediate action)
- HR >120 = **tachycardia** (something is wrong)
- BP >160/90 = **hypertensive crisis** (investigate immediately)
- Confusion = **signs of hypoxia or low BP** (do not ignore)

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Use visual dashboards to show "normal" vs. "critical" vital sign ranges
2. Emphasize that numbers matter — students must communicate exact values
3. Connect vital signs to patient outcomes (SpO2 drop → altered consciousness)
4. Practice saying numbers clearly: "eighty-seven **percent**," not "eighty-seven"

**Common Mistakes to Correct:**
- ❌ "His oxygen is low" → ✅ "SpO2 is 87 percent"
- ❌ "Heart is beating fast" → ✅ "Heart rate is 128"
- ❌ Being vague about numbers → ✅ Always state exact values

**Interactive Extension:**
- Create wall posters with vital sign ranges
- Have students quiz each other on when escalation is needed
- Role-play: "Call me when this patient needs help" scenarios

---

### **Lesson 2: Key Phrases in Action — Red Flags & Urgency**
**Duration:** 12 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Up

**Learning Goal:** Students use assertive, urgent language without panic.

**Key Distinction:** Tone matters as much as words.

| ❌ Panic | ✅ Urgent | ❌ Too Calm |
|----------|-----------|-----------|
| "OH MY GOD! THE PATIENT IS DYING!" | "I'm concerned about deterioration. We need help NOW." | "The patient seems a bit off. Maybe we could check on him later?" |
| Confuses team, wastes time | Clear, actionable, professional | Dangerously delays care |

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Play audio examples — have students identify the difference
2. Practice the "calm urgency" tone (assertive but not panicked)
3. Teach escalation as a **professional responsibility**, not an emotion
4. Role-play: Deliver escalation call with proper tone

**Vocabulary to Practice:**
- "I'm concerned..." (expressing worry professionally)
- "...is urgent" (stating the urgency level)
- "...critical" (describing the severity)
- "We need help NOW" (calling for action)

**Interactive Extension:**
- Record students; playback and discuss tone
- Practice with different scenarios (mild vs. critical)
- Have students coach each other on delivery

---

### **Lesson 3: Understanding the Situation — Controlled Practice**
**Duration:** 15 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Down

**Learning Goal:** Students analyze deterioration scenarios and respond appropriately.

**Scenario Focus:** SpO2 drops gradually from 96% to 89% during sleep.

**Clinical Teaching Points:**
1. **Gradual vs. Sudden:** Gradual decline = time for intervention; sudden drop = immediate action
2. **First Response:** Wake patient, reposition, increase oxygen (nursing interventions)
3. **When to Escalate:** If SpO2 remains <90% after interventions, call for help
4. **Communication:** State what you've done + what you recommend

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Walk through the scenario step-by-step
2. Ask students: "What would you do first?" → "What would you say?"
3. Emphasize the **nursing assessment** (wake, reposition, increase O2) before escalation
4. Practice the language of graduated response

**Common Mistakes:**
- ❌ Panicking immediately when alarm sounds
- ❌ Not attempting basic interventions first
- ❌ Delaying escalation if SpO2 stays low
- ❌ Being vague when calling for help

**Interactive Extension:**
- Simulate monitor alarms with sound
- Have students practice "thinking aloud" (narrate what they would do)
- Practice the 90-second escalation call structure

---

### **Lesson 4: A Second Scenario — Blood Pressure Crisis**
**Duration:** 15 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Down

**Learning Goal:** Students recognize and escalate hypertensive crises.

**Scenario Focus:** Post-op patient, BP spikes from 120/75 → 165/95; patient pale, agitated.

**Clinical Teaching Points:**
1. **Post-op Hypertension:** Common but needs immediate assessment
2. **Differential Diagnosis:** Pain? Medication? Neurological event?
3. **Assessment Before Escalation:** Check patient assessment + vital signs + last medication
4. **Escalation Language:** "BP spiking...I suspect...we need to..."

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Teach blood pressure language: "systolic 165, diastolic 95" (not just "165 over 95")
2. Connect BP spike to patient presentation (pale, agitated)
3. Practice the "educated guess" without certainty ("I suspect pain response")
4. Emphasize that nurse brings clinical observations + vitals to doctor

**Vocabulary to Practice:**
- "Blood pressure is rising rapidly"
- "Systolic [number], diastolic [number]"
- "Patient is pale and agitated"
- "I suspect..."
- "We need to review medications"

**Interactive Extension:**
- Show vital sign trends (graph showing BP rise over time)
- Practice explaining possible causes in clear English
- Role-play: Nurse reporting to doctor + doctor asking clarifying questions

---

### **Lesson 5: Your Turn to Speak — Open Deterioration Scenario**
**Duration:** 18 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Down

**Learning Goal:** Students respond to complex, multi-system deterioration independently.

**Scenario Focus:** Multiple vital changes simultaneously
- SpO2: 94% → 88%
- HR: normal → 115
- BP: 120/70 → 140/88
- Mental state: alert → confused
- Breathing: normal → labored

**Student Task:** Assess and escalate within 2 minutes

**Teaching Tips:**
1. This is **deliberately complex** — it's realistic
2. Emphasize: Don't panic, speak clearly, give actionable information
3. Use SBAR framework if possible (excellent preparation for Lesson 8)
4. Accept "organized panic" — urgency without incoherence is the goal

**Rubric for Assessment:**
- ✅ States all major vital changes clearly
- ✅ Connects findings to clinical urgency
- ✅ Calls for specific help (not vague requests)
- ✅ Speaks in organized, logical flow
- ✅ Uses professional medical terminology

**Interactive Extension:**
- Have students listen to each other's recordings
- Provide peer feedback using the same rubric
- Discuss what made successful escalations clear vs. confusing

---

### **Lesson 6: Pair Practice — Round 1: Structured Escalation**
**Duration:** 20 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Together

**Learning Goal:** Students practice escalation dialogue with peers using full scripts.

**Dialogue Pattern:**
1. **Junior Nurse:** States concern, vital signs, what's been done
2. **Senior Nurse:** Acknowledges, gives orders, ensures continuity

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Assign partners carefully (mix proficiency levels)
2. Have students rotate roles (junior ↔ senior)
3. Emphasize: Junior nurse's job is to **communicate clearly**, not diagnose
4. Senior nurse's job is to **respond decisively** and **give next steps**

**Key Language:**
- Junior: "I'm concerned...SpO2 has dropped...I've already..."
- Senior: "I'm coming...While I'm on my way, prepare...Keep monitoring..."

**Common Mistakes to Correct:**
- ❌ Junior nurse being too timid ("Maybe you could check sometime?")
- ❌ Junior nurse forgetting vital signs numbers
- ❌ Senior nurse dismissing concerns without acknowledgment
- ❌ Communication breaking down mid-escalation

**Interactive Extension:**
- Record dialogues and discuss communication quality
- Add realistic "obstacles" (phone delays, background noise)
- Practice escalation with different nurse relationships (colleague, senior, charge nurse)

---

### **Lesson 7: Pair Practice — Round 2: Responding to Family Anxiety**
**Duration:** 20 minutes | **Stage:** Heads Together

**Learning Goal:** Students communicate with frightened family members while being honest.

**Dialogue Pattern:**
1. **Anxious Family Member:** Expresses fear, asks questions, needs reassurance
2. **Nurse:** Balances honesty with reassurance, explains actions being taken

**Teaching Tips:**
1. Emphasize: **Empathy first, then information**
2. Use clear language — explain what's happening without medical jargon
3. Acknowledge fear: "I know this is frightening"
4. Explain actions: "Here's what we're doing..."
5. Offer hope: "We catch these changes early"

**What to Say:**
- ✅ "Your mother's condition has changed"
- ✅ "I want to be honest with you"
- ✅ "We're monitoring her very carefully"
- ✅ "We're taking steps to help"

**What NOT to Say:**
- ❌ "Everything is fine" (dishonest)
- ❌ "She might die" (unnecessarily alarming)
- ❌ Medical jargon without explanation
- ❌ Making promises ("She'll definitely recover")

**Common Mistakes:**
- ❌ Being too clinical or distant
- ❌ Overusing medical terminology
- ❌ Rushing the conversation
- ❌ Avoiding the family's emotions

**Interactive Extension:**
- Role-play with different family dynamics (angry, scared, demanding)
- Have students discuss what made them feel reassured in past interactions
- Practice difficult conversations: "What if family doesn't trust us?"

### **Lesson 8: Module Assessment — Mixed Input & Self-Reflection**
**Duration:** 25 minutes | **Stage:** Assessment

**Learning Goal:** Comprehensive assessment of escalation skills + metacognitive reflection.

**Assessment Components:**

#### 1. Quiz (4 Questions - ~10 minutes)
- Critical decision-making under time pressure
- SBAR framework knowledge
- Clinical significance of vital signs
- Appropriate urgency levels

**Teaching Notes:**
- These are not "gotcha" questions — they test real critical thinking
- Explain answers thoroughly; students should understand the "why"
- Use as teaching moment, not just assessment

#### 2. Cloze Activity (5-8 blanks - ~5 minutes)
- Complete a full SBAR handover with missing words
- Tests integrated vocabulary knowledge
- Student fills in: Situation → Background → Assessment → Recommendation

**Teaching Notes:**
- Demonstrate with first SBAR before student attempts
- Show how information flows logically
- Accept reasonable alternatives if they make clinical sense

#### 3. Final Recording Submission (~8 minutes)
- **Scenario:** COPD exacerbation patient deteriorating rapidly
- **Task:** Record a complete escalation call (150 seconds)
- **Rubric:**
  - SBAR structure present?
  - Vital signs stated accurately & clearly?
  - Clinical language used appropriately?
  - Urgency conveyed without panic?
  - Recommendations action-oriented?

**Teaching Tips:**
- This is the "capstone" of the module — it's challenging
- Give practice time before recording
- Emphasize: Don't aim for perfection, aim for **clarity + professionalism**
- Accept second takes if needed

#### 4. Self-Reflection (~5 minutes)
- 5 emoji-scale questions (1-5):
  - Clarity of explanations
  - Confidence under pressure
  - Completeness of communication
  - Urgency balance (not too calm, not too panicked)
  - Clinical language use
- Open text: "What was your biggest challenge? What do you want to practice more?"

**Teaching Notes:**
- This **must not feel like grading** — it's reflection
- Students' self-awareness > perfect scores
- Use feedback to inform next module's focus areas
- Celebrate honest self-assessment

## 🎓 Assessment & Grading Strategy

### Formative Assessment (Lessons 1-7)
- Quiz questions in individual lessons are **teaching tools**, not just assessment
- Provide immediate feedback
- Celebrate incremental progress
- Use errors as teaching moments

### Summative Assessment (Lesson 8)

| Component | Weight | Criteria |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| **Quiz (4 Q)** | 20% | Correct answers + understanding of clinical reasoning |
| **Cloze (SBAR)** | 20% | Accurate word selection + logical flow |
| **Final Recording** | 50% | SBAR structure, clarity, clinical language, urgency, professionalism |
| **Self-Reflection** | 10% | Honesty + metacognitive awareness |

**Passing Threshold:** 70% overall

**Remediation Path:**
- If quiz <60%: Review vital signs & SBAR framework
- If recording <60%: Practice one-on-one escalation dialogues
- If self-reflection shows low confidence: Extra pair practice

## 💡 Teaching Tips & Best Practices

### 1. **Make It Real**
- Connect to real hospital protocols your students know
- Use actual vital sign ranges from their workplace
- Invite guest speakers: nurses, doctors, family members who've been in emergencies

### 2. **Use Audio Strategically**
- Play audio once at normal speed (comprehension)
- Play again at slow speed (repetition, pronunciation)
- Have students repeat after pauses
- Discuss: "How did that nurse sound? Why?"

### 3. **Practice Escalation Tone**
- Record students and play back
- Discuss: "Did you sound calm? Urgent? Professional?"
- Practice with mirror if needed
- Normalize imperfection — escalation is hard!

### 4. **Group Role-Plays**
- Lesson 6-7 work better with actual peers (not solo recording)
- Assign roles: nurse, doctor, family member, observer
- Observer gives feedback: "What was clear? What was confusing?"
- Rotate roles so everyone practices both sides

### 5. **Connect to Module 1**
- Remind students: Module 1 was "first contact" — Module 5 is "escalation"
- Show how initial assessment skills feed into deterioration communication
- Build narrative: Patient journey from initial contact → crisis → escalation

### 6. **Address Cultural Context**
- Family communication differs across cultures
- Vietnamese families may have different expectations of honesty
- Discuss: How do you balance truth-telling with face-saving?
- Practice phrases that are both honest and respectful

### 7. **Manage Emotional Content**
- This module deals with serious, sometimes distressing scenarios
- Some students may have lived through emergencies
- Create safe space for processing emotions
- Debrief after intense role-plays

## 📝 Common Questions Students Ask

**Q: "What if I forget what to say in an emergency?"**  
**A:** "That's why we practice! SBAR is your framework — it's like a checklist. Situation → Background → Assessment → Recommendation. Even under stress, you can remember: *Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation*."

**Q: "How do I avoid sounding panicked?"**  
**A:** "Practice is the answer. Also: breathe, speak clearly, give facts (not emotions). The medical team NEEDS facts. Your calmness helps them stay calm."

**Q: "What if I'm wrong about what's happening?"**  
**A:** "That's okay! Your job is to report what you see and escalate. The doctor confirms whether you're right. You're raising the alarm, not making the diagnosis. Raise the alarm confidently."

**Q: "How honest should I be with family?"**  
**A:** "Tell the truth, but also tell them what you're doing about it. Not: 'Your mother might die.' Rather: 'Her oxygen is low, so we're giving her more oxygen, and the doctor is examining her.' Same truth, different focus."

## 🚀 Extension Activities

### **Option 1: Simulated Emergency Drill**
- Invite a simulated patient (actor or mannequin)
- Create realistic emergency scenario
- Have students rotate through roles
- Debrief: What went well? What was hard?

### **Option 2: Guest Speaker Panel**
- Invite: ICU nurse, ER doctor, respiratory therapist, family member of emergency patient
- Each speaks about their perspective
- Students ask questions
- Discussion: How do they communicate during emergencies?

### **Option 3: Write a Case Study**
- Student writes a realistic deterioration case
- Peer reads it and records escalation call
- Original student gives feedback
- Publish best cases on course portal

### **Option 4: Journal Reflection**
- Students write about a real emergency they've experienced
- Reflect: "What language did I use? What would I say differently?"
- Optional: Share with class
- Normalize: Emergency communication is learned and refined, not perfect

### **Option 5: Vocabulary Mastery Challenge**
- Create wall of vital sign numbers
- Students point and say phrases: "SpO2 is 87 percent"
- Race to see who can do it fastest and most clearly
- Gamify: Leaderboard of top escalators

## 📊 Student Success Indicators

**Students are succeeding if they can:**

✅ Name vital sign changes quickly and accurately  
✅ Recognize when a situation is urgent without instruction  
✅ Deliver a 90-second escalation call with 3+ vital signs  
✅ Use SBAR without prompting  
✅ Speak to anxious family with empathy + clarity  
✅ Receive feedback and adjust their communication  
✅ Reflect honestly on their own communication quality  
✅ Coach peers on escalation language  

**If students are struggling:**
- ❌ Forgetting vital sign numbers → Practice saying numbers daily
- ❌ Sounding panicked → Listen to experts, practice tone
- ❌ Forgetting SBAR structure → Use a physical checklist
- ❌ Unable to explain situation clearly → Slow down, one vital sign at a time
- ❌ Low confidence → Extra pair practice; focus on courage, not perfection
